**Cryptography**, or **cryptology**,[[1]](#cite_note-1) is the practice and study of techniques for [secure communication](/wiki/Secure_communication "Secure communication") in the presence of [adversarial](/wiki/Adversary_(cryptography) "Adversary (cryptography)") behavior.[[2]](#cite_note-2) More generally, cryptography is about constructing and analyzing [protocols](/wiki/Communication_protocol "Communication protocol") that prevent third parties or the public from reading private messages.[[3]](#cite_note-3) Modern cryptography exists at the intersection of the disciplines of [mathematics](/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [computer science](/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science"), [information security](/wiki/Information_security "Information security"), [electrical engineering](/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering"), [digital signal processing](/wiki/Digital_signal_processing "Digital signal processing"), physics, and others.[[4]](#cite_note-4) Core concepts related to [information security](/wiki/Information_security "Information security") ([data confidentiality](/wiki/Confidentiality "Confidentiality"), [data integrity](/wiki/Data_integrity "Data integrity"), [authentication](/wiki/Authentication "Authentication") and [non-repudiation](/wiki/Non-repudiation "Non-repudiation")) are also central to cryptography.[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5) Practical applications of cryptography include [electronic commerce](/wiki/Electronic_commerce "Electronic commerce"), [chip-based payment cards](/wiki/Smart_card#EMV "Smart card"), [digital currencies](/wiki/Digital_currencies "Digital currencies"), [computer passwords](/wiki/Password "Password") and [military communications](/wiki/Military_communications "Military communications").

Cryptography prior to the modern age was effectively synonymous with [encryption](/wiki/Encryption "Encryption"), converting readable information ([plaintext](/wiki/Plaintext "Plaintext")) to unintelligible [nonsense](/wiki/Nonsense "Nonsense") text ([ciphertext](/wiki/Ciphertext "Ciphertext")), which can only be read by reversing the process ([decryption](/wiki/Decryption "Decryption")). The sender of an encrypted (coded) message shares the decryption (decoding) technique only with the intended recipients to preclude access from adversaries. The cryptography literature [often uses the names](/wiki/Alice_and_Bob "Alice and Bob") "Alice" (or "A") for the sender, "Bob" (or "B") for the intended recipient, and "Eve" (or "E") for the [eavesdropping](/wiki/Eavesdropping "Eavesdropping") adversary.[[6]](#cite_note-Biggs-2008-6) Since the development of [rotor cipher machines](/wiki/Rotor_machine "Rotor machine") in [World War I](/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") and the advent of computers in [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), cryptography methods have become increasingly complex and their applications more varied.

Modern cryptography is heavily based on [mathematical theory](/wiki/Mathematical_theory "Mathematical theory") and computer science practice; cryptographic [algorithms](/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") are designed around [computational hardness assumptions](/wiki/Computational_hardness_assumption "Computational hardness assumption"), making such algorithms hard to break in actual practice by any adversary. While it is theoretically possible to break into a well-designed system, it is infeasible in actual practice to do so. Such schemes, if well designed, are therefore termed "computationally secure". Theoretical advances (e.g., improvements in [integer factorization](/wiki/Integer_factorization "Integer factorization") algorithms) and faster computing technology require these designs to be continually reevaluated and, if necessary, adapted. [Information-theoretically secure](/wiki/Information-theoretic_security "Information-theoretic security") schemes that provably cannot be broken even with unlimited computing power, such as the [one-time pad](/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad"), are much more difficult to use in practice than the best theoretically breakable but computationally secure schemes.

The growth of cryptographic technology has raised [a number of legal issues](/wiki/Cryptography_law "Cryptography law") in the [Information Age](/wiki/Information_Age "Information Age"). Cryptography's potential for use as a tool for espionage and [sedition](/wiki/Sedition "Sedition") has led many governments to classify it as a weapon and to limit or even prohibit its use and export.[[7]](#cite_note-Crypto_Law_Survey-2013-7) In some jurisdictions where the use of cryptography is legal, laws permit investigators to [compel the disclosure](/wiki/Key_disclosure_law "Key disclosure law") of [encryption keys](/wiki/Key_(cryptography) "Key (cryptography)") for documents relevant to an investigation.[[8]](#cite_note-PC_World-2007-8)[[9]](#cite_note-Ranger-2015-9) Cryptography also plays a major role in [digital rights management](/wiki/Digital_rights_management "Digital rights management") and [copyright infringement](/wiki/Copyright_infringement "Copyright infringement") disputes with regard to [digital media](/wiki/Digital_media "Digital media").[[10]](#cite_note-Doctorow-2007-10)

## Terminology

[![diagram showing shift three alphabetic cypher D becomes A and E becomes B](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Caesar_cipher_left_shift_of_3.svg/250px-Caesar_cipher_left_shift_of_3.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Caesar_cipher_left_shift_of_3.svg)

Alphabet shift ciphers are believed to have been used by [Julius Caesar](/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") over 2,000 years ago.[[6]](#cite_note-Biggs-2008-6) This is an example with *k* = 3. In other words, the letters in the alphabet are shifted three in one direction to encrypt and three in the other direction to decrypt.

The first use of the term "cryptograph" (as opposed to "[cryptogram](/wiki/Cryptogram "Cryptogram")") dates back to the 19th century – originating from "[The Gold-Bug](/wiki/The_Gold-Bug "The Gold-Bug")", a story by [Edgar Allan Poe](/wiki/Edgar_Allan_Poe "Edgar Allan Poe").[[11]](#cite_note-11)[[12]](#cite_note-12) The etymology of the term goes back to the [Greek language](/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language"); it is formed by two constituents: "crypton" (hidden), and "grapho" (to write).

Until modern times, cryptography referred almost exclusively to "encryption", which is the process of converting ordinary information (called [plaintext](/wiki/Plaintext "Plaintext")) into an unintelligible form (called [ciphertext](/wiki/Ciphertext "Ciphertext")).[[13]](#cite_note-Kahn-1967-13) Decryption is the reverse, in other words, moving from the unintelligible ciphertext back to plaintext. A [cipher](/wiki/Cipher "Cipher") (or cypher) is a pair of algorithms that carry out the encryption and the reversing decryption. The detailed operation of a cipher is controlled both by the algorithm and, in each instance, by a "key". The key is a secret (ideally known only to the communicants), usually a string of characters (ideally short so it can be remembered by the user), which is needed to decrypt the ciphertext. In formal mathematical terms, a "[cryptosystem](/wiki/Cryptosystem "Cryptosystem")" is the ordered list of elements of finite possible plaintexts, finite possible cyphertexts, finite possible keys, and the encryption and decryption algorithms that correspond to each key. Keys are important both formally and in actual practice, as ciphers without variable keys can be trivially broken with only the knowledge of the cipher used and are therefore useless (or even counter-productive) for most purposes. Historically, ciphers were often used directly for encryption or decryption without additional procedures such as [authentication](/wiki/Authentication "Authentication") or integrity checks.

There are two main types of cryptosystems: [symmetric](/wiki/Symmetric-key_algorithm "Symmetric-key algorithm") and [asymmetric](/wiki/Public-key_cryptography "Public-key cryptography"). In symmetric systems, the only ones known until the 1970s, the same secret key encrypts and decrypts a message. Data manipulation in symmetric systems is significantly faster than in asymmetric systems. Asymmetric systems use a "public key" to encrypt a message and a related "private key" to decrypt it. The advantage of asymmetric systems is that the public key can be freely published, allowing parties to establish secure communication without having a shared secret key. In practice, asymmetric systems are used to first exchange a secret key, and then secure communication proceeds via a more efficient symmetric system using that key.[[14]](#cite_note-14) Examples of asymmetric systems include [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](/wiki/Diffie%E2%80%93Hellman_key_exchange "Diffie–Hellman key exchange"), RSA ([Rivest–Shamir–Adleman](/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem) "RSA (cryptosystem)")), ECC ([Elliptic Curve Cryptography](/wiki/Elliptic_Curve_Cryptography "Elliptic Curve Cryptography")), and [Post-quantum cryptography](/wiki/Post-quantum_cryptography "Post-quantum cryptography"). Secure symmetric algorithms include the commonly used AES ([Advanced Encryption Standard](/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard "Advanced Encryption Standard")) which replaced the older DES ([Data Encryption Standard](/wiki/Data_Encryption_Standard "Data Encryption Standard")).[[15]](#cite_note-15) Insecure symmetric algorithms include children's language tangling schemes such as [Pig Latin](/wiki/Pig_Latin "Pig Latin") or other [cant](/wiki/Cant "Cant"), and all historical cryptographic schemes, however seriously intended, prior to the invention of the [one-time pad](/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad") early in the 20th century.

In [colloquial](/wiki/Colloquial "Colloquial") use, the term "[code](/wiki/Code_(cryptography) "Code (cryptography)")" is often used to mean any method of encryption or concealment of meaning. However, in cryptography, code has a more specific meaning: the replacement of a unit of plaintext (i.e., a meaningful word or phrase) with a code word (for example, "wallaby" replaces "attack at dawn"). A cypher, in contrast, is a scheme for changing or substituting an element below such a level (a letter, a syllable, or a pair of letters, etc.) to produce a cyphertext.

[Cryptanalysis](/wiki/Cryptanalysis "Cryptanalysis") is the term used for the study of methods for obtaining the meaning of encrypted information without access to the key normally required to do so; i.e., it is the study of how to "crack" encryption algorithms or their implementations.

Some use the terms "cryptography" and "cryptology" interchangeably in English,[[16]](#cite_note-16) while others (including US military practice generally) use "cryptography" to refer specifically to the use and practice of cryptographic techniques and "cryptology" to refer to the combined study of cryptography and cryptanalysis.[[17]](#cite_note-17)[[18]](#cite_note-18) English is more flexible than several other languages in which "cryptology" (done by cryptologists) is always used in the second sense above. [RFC](/wiki/RFC_(identifier) "RFC (identifier)") [2828](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2828) advises that [steganography](/wiki/Steganography "Steganography") is sometimes included in cryptology.[[19]](#cite_note-rfc2828-19)

The study of characteristics of languages that have some application in cryptography or cryptology (e.g. frequency data, letter combinations, universal patterns, etc.) is called cryptolinguistics. Cryptolingusitics is especially used in military intelligence applications for deciphering foreign communications.[[20]](#cite_note-20)[[21]](#cite_note-21)

## History

Main article: [History of cryptography](/wiki/History_of_cryptography "History of cryptography")

Before the modern era, cryptography focused on message confidentiality (i.e., encryption)—conversion of [messages](/wiki/Information "Information") from a comprehensible form into an incomprehensible one and back again at the other end, rendering it unreadable by interceptors or [eavesdroppers](/wiki/Eavesdropper "Eavesdropper") without secret knowledge (namely the key needed for decryption of that message). Encryption attempted to ensure [secrecy](/wiki/Secrecy "Secrecy") in communication, such as those of [spies](/wiki/Spy "Spy"), military leaders, and diplomats. In recent decades, the field has expanded beyond confidentiality concerns to include techniques for message integrity checking, sender/receiver identity authentication, [digital signatures](/wiki/Digital_signature "Digital signature"), [interactive proofs](/wiki/Interactive_proof_system "Interactive proof system") and [secure computation](/wiki/Secure_multiparty_computation "Secure multiparty computation"), among others.

### Classic cryptography

[![Skytala stick with strip of paper wound around in spiral](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Skytala%26EmptyStrip-Shaded.png/250px-Skytala%26EmptyStrip-Shaded.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Skytala%26EmptyStrip-Shaded.png)

Reconstructed [ancient Greek](/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek") *[scytale](/wiki/Scytale "Scytale")*, an early cipher device

The main classical cipher types are [transposition ciphers](/wiki/Transposition_cipher "Transposition cipher"), which rearrange the order of letters in a message (e.g., 'hello world' becomes 'ehlol owrdl' in a trivially simple rearrangement scheme), and [substitution ciphers](/wiki/Substitution_cipher "Substitution cipher"), which systematically replace letters or groups of letters with other letters or groups of letters (e.g., 'fly at once' becomes 'gmz bu podf' by replacing each letter with the one following it in the [Latin alphabet](/wiki/Latin_alphabet "Latin alphabet")).[[22]](#cite_note-22) Simple versions of either have never offered much confidentiality from enterprising opponents. An early substitution cipher was the [Caesar cipher](/wiki/Caesar_cipher "Caesar cipher"), in which each letter in the plaintext was replaced by a letter three positions further down the alphabet.[[23]](#cite_note-23) [Suetonius](/wiki/Suetonius "Suetonius") reports that [Julius Caesar](/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") used it with a shift of three to communicate with his generals. [Atbash](/wiki/Atbash "Atbash") is an example of an early Hebrew cipher. The earliest known use of cryptography is some carved ciphertext on stone in [Egypt](/wiki/Egypt "Egypt") (c. 1900 BCE), but this may have been done for the amusement of literate observers rather than as a way of concealing information.

The [Greeks of Classical times](/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") are said to have known of ciphers (e.g., the [scytale](/wiki/Scytale "Scytale") transposition cipher claimed to have been used by the [Spartan](/wiki/Sparta "Sparta") military).[[24]](#cite_note-24) [Steganography](/wiki/Steganography "Steganography") (i.e., hiding even the existence of a message so as to keep it confidential) was also first developed in ancient times. An early example, from [Herodotus](/wiki/Herodotus "Herodotus"), was a message tattooed on a slave's shaved head and concealed under the regrown hair.[[13]](#cite_note-Kahn-1967-13) Other steganography methods involve 'hiding in plain sight,' such as using a [music cipher](/wiki/Music_cipher#Musical_Steganography "Music cipher") to disguise an encrypted message within a regular piece of sheet music. More modern examples of steganography include the use of [invisible ink](/wiki/Invisible_ink "Invisible ink"), [microdots](/wiki/Microdot "Microdot"), and [digital watermarks](/wiki/Digital_watermark "Digital watermark") to conceal information.

In India, the 2000-year-old *[Kama Sutra](/wiki/Kama_Sutra "Kama Sutra")* of [Vātsyāyana](/wiki/V%C4%81tsy%C4%81yana "Vātsyāyana") speaks of two different kinds of ciphers called Kautiliyam and Mulavediya. In the Kautiliyam, the cipher letter substitutions are based on phonetic relations, such as vowels becoming consonants. In the Mulavediya, the cipher alphabet consists of pairing letters and using the reciprocal ones.[[13]](#cite_note-Kahn-1967-13)

In [Sassanid Persia](/wiki/Sassanid_Persia "Sassanid Persia"), there were two secret scripts, according to the Muslim author [Ibn al-Nadim](/wiki/Ibn_al-Nadim "Ibn al-Nadim"): the *šāh-dabīrīya* (literally "King's script") which was used for official correspondence, and the *rāz-saharīya* which was used to communicate secret messages with other countries.[[25]](#cite_note-25)

[David Kahn](/wiki/David_Kahn_(writer) "David Kahn (writer)") notes in *[The Codebreakers](/wiki/The_Codebreakers "The Codebreakers")* that modern cryptology originated among the [Arabs](/wiki/Arabs "Arabs"), the first people to systematically document cryptanalytic methods.[[26]](#cite_note-26) [Al-Khalil](/wiki/Al-Khalil_ibn_Ahmad_al-Farahidi "Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi") (717–786) wrote the *Book of Cryptographic Messages*, which contains the first use of [permutations and combinations](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/permutation "wikt:permutation") to list all possible Arabic words with and without vowels.[[27]](#cite_note-27)

[![Arabic text of a book by Al-Kindi](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Al-kindi_cryptographic.png/250px-Al-kindi_cryptographic.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Al-kindi_cryptographic.png)

First page of a book by [Al-Kindi](/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi") which discusses encryption of messages

Ciphertexts produced by a [classical cipher](/wiki/Classical_cipher "Classical cipher") (and some modern ciphers) will reveal statistical information about the plaintext, and that information can often be used to break the cipher. After the discovery of [frequency analysis](/wiki/Frequency_analysis "Frequency analysis"), nearly all such ciphers could be broken by an informed attacker.[[28]](#cite_note-28) Such classical ciphers still enjoy popularity today, though mostly as [puzzles](/wiki/Puzzle "Puzzle") (see [cryptogram](/wiki/Cryptogram "Cryptogram")). The [Arab mathematician](/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam "Mathematics in medieval Islam") and [polymath](/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") Al-Kindi wrote a book on cryptography entitled *Risalah fi Istikhraj al-Mu'amma* (*Manuscript for the Deciphering Cryptographic Messages*), which described the first known use of frequency analysis cryptanalysis techniques.[[29]](#cite_note-29)[[30]](#cite_note-Al-Kadi-1992-30)

16th-century book-shaped French cipher machine, with arms of [Henri II of France](/wiki/Henri_II_of_France "Henri II of France")

[![manuscript from Gabriel de Luetz d'Aramon in bound volume](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b8/Encoded_letter_of_Gabriel_Luetz_d_Aramon_after_1546_with_partial_deciphering.jpg/250px-Encoded_letter_of_Gabriel_Luetz_d_Aramon_after_1546_with_partial_deciphering.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Encoded_letter_of_Gabriel_Luetz_d_Aramon_after_1546_with_partial_deciphering.jpg)

Enciphered letter from [Gabriel de Luetz d'Aramon](/wiki/Gabriel_de_Luetz_d%27Aramon "Gabriel de Luetz d'Aramon"), [French Ambassador to the Ottoman Empire](/wiki/French_Ambassador_to_the_Ottoman_Empire "French Ambassador to the Ottoman Empire"), after 1546, with partial decipherment

Language letter frequencies may offer little help for some extended historical encryption techniques such as [homophonic cipher](/wiki/Substitution_cipher#Homophonic "Substitution cipher") that tend to flatten the frequency distribution. For those ciphers, language letter group (or n-gram) frequencies may provide an attack.

Essentially all ciphers remained vulnerable to cryptanalysis using the frequency analysis technique until the development of the [polyalphabetic cipher](/wiki/Polyalphabetic_cipher "Polyalphabetic cipher"), most clearly by [Leon Battista Alberti](/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti "Leon Battista Alberti") around the year 1467, though there is some indication that it was already known to Al-Kindi.[[30]](#cite_note-Al-Kadi-1992-30) Alberti's innovation was to use different ciphers (i.e., substitution alphabets) for various parts of a message (perhaps for each successive plaintext letter at the limit). He also invented what was probably the first automatic [cipher device](/wiki/Alberti_Cipher_Disk "Alberti Cipher Disk"), a wheel that implemented a partial realization of his invention. In the [Vigenère cipher](/wiki/Vigen%C3%A8re_cipher "Vigenère cipher"), a [polyalphabetic cipher](/wiki/Polyalphabetic_cipher "Polyalphabetic cipher"), encryption uses a *key word*, which controls letter substitution depending on which letter of the key word is used. In the mid-19th century [Charles Babbage](/wiki/Charles_Babbage "Charles Babbage") showed that the Vigenère cipher was vulnerable to [Kasiski examination](/wiki/Kasiski_examination "Kasiski examination"), but this was first published about ten years later by [Friedrich Kasiski](/wiki/Friedrich_Kasiski "Friedrich Kasiski").[[31]](#cite_note-31)

Although frequency analysis can be a powerful and general technique against many ciphers, encryption has still often been effective in practice, as many a would-be cryptanalyst was unaware of the technique. Breaking a message without using frequency analysis essentially required knowledge of the cipher used and perhaps of the key involved, thus making espionage, bribery, burglary, defection, etc., more attractive approaches to the cryptanalytically uninformed. It was finally explicitly recognized in the 19th century that secrecy of a cipher's algorithm is not a sensible nor practical safeguard of message security; in fact, it was further realized that any adequate cryptographic scheme (including ciphers) should remain secure even if the adversary fully understands the cipher algorithm itself. Security of the key used should alone be sufficient for a good cipher to maintain confidentiality under an attack. This fundamental principle was first explicitly stated in 1883 by [Auguste Kerckhoffs](/wiki/Auguste_Kerckhoffs "Auguste Kerckhoffs") and is generally called [Kerckhoffs's Principle](/wiki/Kerckhoffs%27s_Principle "Kerckhoffs's Principle"); alternatively and more bluntly, it was restated by [Claude Shannon](/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon"), the inventor of [information theory](/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory") and the fundamentals of theoretical cryptography, as *Shannon's Maxim*—'the enemy knows the system'.

Different physical devices and aids have been used to assist with ciphers. One of the earliest may have been the scytale of ancient Greece, a rod supposedly used by the Spartans as an aid for a transposition cipher. In medieval times, other aids were invented such as the [cipher grille](/wiki/Grille_(cryptography) "Grille (cryptography)"), which was also used for a kind of steganography. With the invention of polyalphabetic ciphers came more sophisticated aids such as Alberti's own [cipher disk](/wiki/Cipher_disk "Cipher disk"), [Johannes Trithemius](/wiki/Johannes_Trithemius "Johannes Trithemius")' [tabula recta](/wiki/Tabula_recta "Tabula recta") scheme, and [Thomas Jefferson](/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson "Thomas Jefferson")'s [wheel cypher](/wiki/Jefferson_disk "Jefferson disk") (not publicly known, and reinvented independently by [Bazeries](/wiki/Bazeries "Bazeries") around 1900). Many mechanical encryption/decryption devices were invented early in the 20th century, and several patented, among them [rotor machines](/wiki/Rotor_machine "Rotor machine")—famously including the [Enigma machine](/wiki/Enigma_machine "Enigma machine") used by the German government and military from the late 1920s and during [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").[[32]](#cite_note-32) The ciphers implemented by better quality examples of these machine designs brought about a substantial increase in cryptanalytic difficulty after WWI.[[33]](#cite_note-33)

### Early computer-era cryptography

Cryptanalysis of the new mechanical ciphering devices proved to be both difficult and laborious. In the United Kingdom, cryptanalytic efforts at [Bletchley Park](/wiki/Bletchley_Park "Bletchley Park") during WWII spurred the development of more efficient means for carrying out repetitive tasks, such [as military code breaking (decryption)](/wiki/Code-breaking "Code-breaking"). This culminated in the development of the [Colossus](/wiki/Colossus_computer "Colossus computer"), the world's first fully electronic, digital, [programmable](/wiki/Computer_programming "Computer programming") computer, which assisted in the decryption of ciphers generated by the German Army's [Lorenz SZ40/42](/wiki/Lorenz_SZ40/42 "Lorenz SZ40/42") machine.

Extensive open academic research into cryptography is relatively recent, beginning in the mid-1970s. In the early 1970s [IBM](/wiki/IBM "IBM") personnel designed the Data Encryption Standard (DES) algorithm that became the first federal government cryptography standard in the United States.[[34]](#cite_note-34) In 1976 [Whitfield Diffie](/wiki/Whitfield_Diffie "Whitfield Diffie") and [Martin Hellman](/wiki/Martin_Hellman "Martin Hellman") published the Diffie–Hellman key exchange algorithm.[[35]](#cite_note-Diffie-1976-35) In 1977 the [RSA algorithm](/wiki/RSA_(algorithm) "RSA (algorithm)") was published in [Martin Gardner](/wiki/Martin_Gardner "Martin Gardner")'s *[Scientific American](/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American")* column.[[36]](#cite_note-36) Since then, cryptography has become a widely used tool in communications, [computer networks](/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"), and [computer security](/wiki/Computer_security "Computer security") generally.

Some modern cryptographic techniques can only keep their keys secret if certain mathematical problems are [intractable](/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory#Intractability "Computational complexity theory"), such as the [integer factorization](/wiki/Integer_factorization "Integer factorization") or the [discrete logarithm](/wiki/Discrete_logarithm "Discrete logarithm") problems, so there are deep connections with [abstract mathematics](/wiki/Abstract_mathematics "Abstract mathematics"). There are very few cryptosystems that are proven to be unconditionally secure. The [one-time pad](/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad") is one, and was proven to be so by Claude Shannon. There are a few important algorithms that have been proven secure under certain assumptions. For example, the infeasibility of factoring extremely large integers is the basis for believing that RSA is secure, and some other systems, but even so, proof of unbreakability is unavailable since the underlying mathematical problem remains open. In practice, these are widely used, and are believed unbreakable in practice by most competent observers. There are systems similar to RSA, such as one by [Michael O. Rabin](/wiki/Michael_O._Rabin "Michael O. Rabin") that are provably secure provided factoring *n = pq* is impossible; it is quite unusable in practice. The [discrete logarithm problem](/wiki/Discrete_logarithm_problem "Discrete logarithm problem") is the basis for believing some other cryptosystems are secure, and again, there are related, less practical systems that are provably secure relative to the solvability or insolvability discrete log problem.[[37]](#cite_note-37)

As well as being aware of cryptographic history, [cryptographic algorithm](/wiki/Cryptographic_algorithm "Cryptographic algorithm") and system designers must also sensibly consider probable future developments while working on their designs. For instance, continuous improvements in computer processing power have increased the scope of [brute-force attacks](/wiki/Brute-force_attack "Brute-force attack"), so when specifying [key lengths](/wiki/Key_length "Key length"), the required key lengths are similarly advancing.[[38]](#cite_note-38) The potential impact of [quantum computing](/wiki/Quantum_computing "Quantum computing") are already being considered by some cryptographic system designers developing post-quantum cryptography.[*[when?](/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")*] The announced imminence of small implementations of these machines may be making the need for preemptive caution rather more than merely speculative.[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5)

## Modern cryptography

[Claude Shannon](/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon")'s two papers, his [1948 paper](/wiki/A_Mathematical_Theory_of_Communication "A Mathematical Theory of Communication") on [information theory](/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory"), and especially his [1949 paper](/wiki/Communication_Theory_of_Secrecy_Systems "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems") on cryptography, laid the foundations of modern cryptography and provided a mathematical basis for future cryptography.[[39]](#cite_note-39)[[40]](#cite_note-40) His 1949 paper has been noted as having provided a "solid theoretical basis for cryptography and for cryptanalysis",[[41]](#cite_note-41) and as having turned cryptography from an "art to a science".[[42]](#cite_note-42) As a result of his contributions and work, he has been described as the "founding father of modern cryptography".[[43]](#cite_note-43)

Prior to the early 20th century, cryptography was mainly concerned with [linguistic](/wiki/Language "Language") and [lexicographic](/wiki/Lexicographic_code "Lexicographic code") patterns. Since then cryptography has broadened in scope, and now makes extensive use of mathematical subdisciplines, including information theory, [computational complexity](/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory "Computational complexity theory"), statistics, [combinatorics](/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics"), [abstract algebra](/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"), [number theory](/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), and [finite mathematics](/wiki/Finite_mathematics "Finite mathematics").[[44]](#cite_note-44) Cryptography is also a branch of engineering, but an unusual one since it deals with active, intelligent, and malevolent opposition; other kinds of engineering (e.g., civil or chemical engineering) need deal only with neutral natural forces. There is also active research examining the relationship between cryptographic problems and [quantum physics](/wiki/Quantum_physics "Quantum physics").

Just as the development of digital computers and electronics helped in cryptanalysis, it made possible much more complex ciphers. Furthermore, computers allowed for the encryption of any kind of data representable in any binary format, unlike classical ciphers which only encrypted written language texts; this was new and significant. Computer use has thus supplanted linguistic cryptography, both for cipher design and cryptanalysis. Many computer ciphers can be characterized by their operation on [binary](/wiki/Binary_numeral_system "Binary numeral system") [bit](/wiki/Bit "Bit") sequences (sometimes in groups or blocks), unlike classical and mechanical schemes, which generally manipulate traditional characters (i.e., letters and digits) directly. However, computers have also assisted cryptanalysis, which has compensated to some extent for increased cipher complexity. Nonetheless, good modern ciphers have stayed ahead of cryptanalysis; it is typically the case that use of a quality cipher is very efficient (i.e., fast and requiring few resources, such as memory or CPU capability), while breaking it requires an effort many orders of magnitude larger, and vastly larger than that required for any classical cipher, making cryptanalysis so inefficient and impractical as to be effectively impossible.

Research into **post-quantum cryptography** (PQC) has intensified because practical quantum computers would break widely deployed public-key systems such as RSA, Diffie–Hellman and ECC. A 2017 review in *Nature* surveys the leading PQC families—lattice-based, code-based, multivariate-quadratic and hash-based schemes—and stresses that standardisation and deployment should proceed well before large-scale quantum machines become available.[[45]](#cite_note-BernsteinLange2017-45)

### Symmetric-key cryptography

Main article: [Symmetric-key algorithm](/wiki/Symmetric-key_algorithm "Symmetric-key algorithm")

[![Diagram showing an encrypt and decrypt process with a key](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Symmetric_key_encryption.svg/330px-Symmetric_key_encryption.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Symmetric_key_encryption.svg)

Symmetric-key cryptography, where a single key is used for both encryption and decryption

Symmetric-key cryptography refers to encryption methods in which both the sender and receiver share the same key (or, less commonly, in which their keys are different, but related in an easily computable way). This was the only kind of encryption publicly known until June 1976.[[35]](#cite_note-Diffie-1976-35)

[![logic diagram showing International Data Encryption Algorithm cypher process](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/International_Data_Encryption_Algorithm_InfoBox_Diagram.svg/250px-International_Data_Encryption_Algorithm_InfoBox_Diagram.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:International_Data_Encryption_Algorithm_InfoBox_Diagram.svg)

One round (out of 8.5) of the [IDEA](/wiki/International_Data_Encryption_Algorithm "International Data Encryption Algorithm") cipher, used in most versions of [PGP](/wiki/Pretty_Good_Privacy "Pretty Good Privacy") and OpenPGP compatible software for time-efficient encryption of messages

Symmetric key ciphers are implemented as either [block ciphers](/wiki/Block_ciphers "Block ciphers") or [stream ciphers](/wiki/Stream_ciphers "Stream ciphers"). A block cipher enciphers input in blocks of plaintext as opposed to individual characters, the input form used by a stream cipher.

The [Data Encryption Standard](/wiki/Data_Encryption_Standard "Data Encryption Standard") (DES) and the [Advanced Encryption Standard](/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard "Advanced Encryption Standard") (AES) are block cipher designs that have been designated [cryptography standards](/wiki/Cryptography_standards "Cryptography standards") by the US government (though DES's designation was finally withdrawn after the AES was adopted).[[46]](#cite_note-46) Despite its deprecation as an official standard, DES (especially its still-approved and much more secure [triple-DES](/wiki/Triple-DES "Triple-DES") variant) remains quite popular; it is used across a wide range of applications, from ATM encryption[[47]](#cite_note-47) to [e-mail privacy](/wiki/E-mail_privacy "E-mail privacy")[[48]](#cite_note-rfc2440-48) and [secure remote access](/wiki/Secure_Shell "Secure Shell").[[49]](#cite_note-49) Many other block ciphers have been designed and released, with considerable variation in quality. Many, even some designed by capable practitioners, have been thoroughly broken, such as [FEAL](/wiki/FEAL "FEAL").[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5)[[50]](#cite_note-Schneier-1996-50)

Stream ciphers, in contrast to the 'block' type, create an arbitrarily long stream of key material, which is combined with the plaintext bit-by-bit or character-by-character, somewhat like the [one-time pad](/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad"). In a stream cipher, the output stream is created based on a hidden internal state that changes as the cipher operates. That internal state is initially set up using the secret key material. [RC4](/wiki/RC4 "RC4") is a widely used stream cipher.[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5) Block ciphers can be used as stream ciphers by generating blocks of a keystream (in place of a [Pseudorandom number generator](/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator "Pseudorandom number generator")) and applying an [XOR](/wiki/Exclusive_or "Exclusive or") operation to each bit of the plaintext with each bit of the keystream.[[51]](#cite_note-51)

[Message authentication codes](/wiki/Message_authentication_code "Message authentication code") (MACs) are much like [cryptographic hash functions](/wiki/Cryptographic_hash_function "Cryptographic hash function"), except that a secret key can be used to authenticate the hash value upon receipt;[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5)[[45]](#cite_note-BernsteinLange2017-45) this additional complication blocks an attack scheme against bare [digest algorithms](/wiki/Md5 "Md5"), and so has been thought worth the effort. Cryptographic hash functions are a third type of cryptographic algorithm. They take a message of any length as input, and output a short, fixed-length [hash](/wiki/Hash_function "Hash function"), which can be used in (for example) a digital signature. For good hash functions, an attacker cannot find two messages that produce the same hash. [MD4](/wiki/MD4 "MD4") is a long-used hash function that is now broken; [MD5](/wiki/MD5 "MD5"), a strengthened variant of MD4, is also widely used but broken in practice. The US [National Security Agency](/wiki/National_Security_Agency "National Security Agency") developed the Secure Hash Algorithm series of MD5-like hash functions: SHA-0 was a flawed algorithm that the agency withdrew; [SHA-1](/wiki/SHA-1 "SHA-1") is widely deployed and more secure than MD5, but cryptanalysts have identified attacks against it; the [SHA-2](/wiki/SHA-2 "SHA-2") family improves on SHA-1, but is vulnerable to clashes as of 2011; and the US standards authority thought it "prudent" from a security perspective to develop a new standard to "significantly improve the robustness of [NIST](/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology "National Institute of Standards and Technology")'s overall hash algorithm toolkit."[[52]](#cite_note-Federal_Register-2007-52) Thus, a [hash function design competition](/wiki/NIST_hash_function_competition "NIST hash function competition") was meant to select a new U.S. national standard, to be called [SHA-3](/wiki/SHA-3 "SHA-3"), by 2012. The competition ended on October 2, 2012, when the NIST announced that [Keccak](/wiki/Keccak "Keccak") would be the new SHA-3 hash algorithm.[[53]](#cite_note-NIST-2012-53) Unlike block and stream ciphers that are invertible, cryptographic hash functions produce a hashed output that cannot be used to retrieve the original input data. Cryptographic hash functions are used to verify the authenticity of data retrieved from an untrusted source or to add a layer of security.

### Public-key cryptography

Main article: [Public-key cryptography](/wiki/Public-key_cryptography "Public-key cryptography")

[![diagram of Public-key cryptography showing public key and private key](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Public_key_encryption.svg/330px-Public_key_encryption.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Public_key_encryption.svg)

Public-key cryptography, where different keys are used for encryption and decryption

Symmetric-key cryptosystems use the same key for encryption and decryption of a message, although a message or group of messages can have a different key than others. A significant disadvantage of symmetric ciphers is the [key management](/wiki/Key_management "Key management") necessary to use them securely. Each distinct pair of communicating parties must, ideally, share a different key, and perhaps for each ciphertext exchanged as well. The number of keys required increases as the [square](/wiki/Square_(algebra) "Square (algebra)") of the number of network members, which very quickly requires complex key management schemes to keep them all consistent and secret.

[![headshots of Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/Diffie_and_Hellman.jpg/250px-Diffie_and_Hellman.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Diffie_and_Hellman.jpg)

[Whitfield Diffie](/wiki/Whitfield_Diffie "Whitfield Diffie") and [Martin Hellman](/wiki/Martin_Hellman "Martin Hellman"), authors of the first published paper on public-key cryptography

In a groundbreaking 1976 paper, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman proposed the notion of *public-key* (also, more generally, called *asymmetric key*) cryptography in which two different but mathematically related keys are used—a *public* key and a *private* key.[[54]](#cite_note-54) A public key system is so constructed that calculation of one key (the 'private key') is computationally infeasible from the other (the 'public key'), even though they are necessarily related. Instead, both keys are generated secretly, as an interrelated pair.[[55]](#cite_note-55) The historian [David Kahn](/wiki/David_Kahn_(writer) "David Kahn (writer)") described public-key cryptography as "the most revolutionary new concept in the field since polyalphabetic substitution emerged in the Renaissance".[[56]](#cite_note-56)

In public-key cryptosystems, the public key may be freely distributed, while its paired private key must remain secret. The *public key* is used for encryption, while the *private* or *secret key* is used for decryption. While Diffie and Hellman could not find such a system, they showed that public-key cryptography was indeed possible by presenting the [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](/wiki/Diffie%E2%80%93Hellman_key_exchange "Diffie–Hellman key exchange") protocol, a solution that is now widely used in secure communications to allow two parties to secretly agree on a [shared encryption key](/wiki/Symmetric-key_algorithm "Symmetric-key algorithm").[[35]](#cite_note-Diffie-1976-35)
The [X.509](/wiki/X.509 "X.509") standard defines the most commonly used format for [public key certificates](/wiki/Public_key_certificates "Public key certificates").[[57]](#cite_note-57)

Diffie and Hellman's publication sparked widespread academic efforts in finding a practical public-key encryption system. This race was finally won in 1978 by [Ronald Rivest](/wiki/Ronald_Rivest "Ronald Rivest"), [Adi Shamir](/wiki/Adi_Shamir "Adi Shamir"), and [Len Adleman](/wiki/Len_Adleman "Len Adleman"), whose solution has since become known as the [RSA algorithm](/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem) "RSA (cryptosystem)").[[58]](#cite_note-58)

The [Diffie–Hellman](/wiki/Diffie%E2%80%93Hellman_key_exchange "Diffie–Hellman key exchange") and [RSA algorithms](/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem) "RSA (cryptosystem)"), in addition to being the first publicly known examples of high-quality public-key algorithms, have been among the most widely used. Other [asymmetric-key algorithms](/wiki/Category:Asymmetric-key_algorithms "Category:Asymmetric-key algorithms") include the [Cramer–Shoup cryptosystem](/wiki/Cramer%E2%80%93Shoup_cryptosystem "Cramer–Shoup cryptosystem"), [ElGamal encryption](/wiki/ElGamal_encryption "ElGamal encryption"), and various [elliptic curve techniques](/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography "Elliptic curve cryptography").

A document published in 1997 by the Government Communications Headquarters ([GCHQ](/wiki/GCHQ "GCHQ")), a British intelligence organization, revealed that cryptographers at GCHQ had anticipated several academic developments.[[59]](#cite_note-Wayner-1997-59) Reportedly, around 1970, [James H. Ellis](/wiki/James_H._Ellis "James H. Ellis") had conceived the principles of asymmetric key cryptography. In 1973, [Clifford Cocks](/wiki/Clifford_Cocks "Clifford Cocks") invented a solution that was very similar in design rationale to RSA.[[59]](#cite_note-Wayner-1997-59)[[60]](#cite_note-60) In 1974, [Malcolm J. Williamson](/wiki/Malcolm_J._Williamson "Malcolm J. Williamson") is claimed to have developed the Diffie–Hellman key exchange.[[61]](#cite_note-61)

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Private_key_signing.svg/250px-Private_key_signing.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Private_key_signing.svg)

In this example the message is only signed and not encrypted. 1) Alice signs a message with her private key. 2) Bob can verify that Alice sent the message and that the message has not been modified.

Public-key cryptography is also used for implementing [digital signature](/wiki/Digital_signature "Digital signature") schemes. A digital signature is reminiscent of an ordinary signature; they both have the characteristic of being easy for a user to produce, but difficult for anyone else to [forge](/wiki/Forgery "Forgery"). Digital signatures can also be permanently tied to the content of the message being signed; they cannot then be 'moved' from one document to another, for any attempt will be detectable. In digital signature schemes, there are two algorithms: one for *signing*, in which a secret key is used to process the message (or a hash of the message, or both), and one for *verification*, in which the matching public key is used with the message to check the validity of the signature. RSA and [DSA](/wiki/Digital_Signature_Algorithm "Digital Signature Algorithm") are two of the most popular digital signature schemes.[[62]](#cite_note-62) Digital signatures are central to the operation of [public key infrastructures](/wiki/Public_key_infrastructure "Public key infrastructure") and many network security schemes (e.g., [SSL/TLS](/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security "Transport Layer Security"), many [VPNs](/wiki/VPN "VPN"), etc.).[[50]](#cite_note-Schneier-1996-50)

Public-key algorithms are most often based on the [computational complexity](/wiki/Computational_complexity_theory "Computational complexity theory") of "hard" problems, often from [number theory](/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"). For example, the hardness of RSA is related to the [integer factorization](/wiki/Integer_factorization "Integer factorization") problem, while Diffie–Hellman and DSA are related to the [discrete logarithm](/wiki/Discrete_logarithm "Discrete logarithm") problem. The security of [elliptic curve cryptography](/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography "Elliptic curve cryptography") is based on number theoretic problems involving [elliptic curves](/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve"). Because of the difficulty of the underlying problems, most public-key algorithms involve operations such as [modular](/wiki/Modular_arithmetic "Modular arithmetic") multiplication and exponentiation, which are much more computationally expensive than the techniques used in most block ciphers, especially with typical key sizes. As a result, public-key cryptosystems are commonly [hybrid cryptosystems](/wiki/Hybrid_cryptosystem "Hybrid cryptosystem"), in which a fast high-quality symmetric-key encryption algorithm is used for the message itself, while the relevant symmetric key is sent with the message, but encrypted using a public-key algorithm. Similarly, hybrid signature schemes are often used, in which a cryptographic hash function is computed, and only the resulting hash is digitally signed.[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5)

### Cryptographic hash functions

Main article: [Cryptographic hash function](/wiki/Cryptographic_hash_function "Cryptographic hash function")

Cryptographic hash functions are functions that take a variable-length input and return a fixed-length output, which can be used in, for example, a digital signature. For a hash function to be secure, it must be difficult to compute two inputs that hash to the same value ([collision resistance](/wiki/Collision_resistance "Collision resistance")) and to compute an input that hashes to a given output ([preimage resistance](/wiki/Preimage_attack "Preimage attack")). [MD4](/wiki/MD4 "MD4") is a long-used hash function that is now broken; [MD5](/wiki/MD5 "MD5"), a strengthened variant of MD4, is also widely used but broken in practice. The US [National Security Agency](/wiki/National_Security_Agency "National Security Agency") developed the Secure Hash Algorithm series of MD5-like hash functions: SHA-0 was a flawed algorithm that the agency withdrew; [SHA-1](/wiki/SHA-1 "SHA-1") is widely deployed and more secure than MD5, but cryptanalysts have identified attacks against it; the [SHA-2](/wiki/SHA-2 "SHA-2") family improves on SHA-1, but is vulnerable to clashes as of 2011; and the US standards authority thought it "prudent" from a security perspective to develop a new standard to "significantly improve the robustness of [NIST](/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology "National Institute of Standards and Technology")'s overall hash algorithm toolkit."[[52]](#cite_note-Federal_Register-2007-52) Thus, a [hash function design competition](/wiki/NIST_hash_function_competition "NIST hash function competition") was meant to select a new U.S. national standard, to be called [SHA-3](/wiki/SHA-3 "SHA-3"), by 2012. The competition ended on October 2, 2012, when the NIST announced that [Keccak](/wiki/Keccak "Keccak") would be the new SHA-3 hash algorithm.[[53]](#cite_note-NIST-2012-53) Unlike block and stream ciphers that are invertible, cryptographic hash functions produce a hashed output that cannot be used to retrieve the original input data. Cryptographic hash functions are used to verify the authenticity of data retrieved from an untrusted source or to add a layer of security.

### Cryptanalysis

Main article: [Cryptanalysis](/wiki/Cryptanalysis "Cryptanalysis")

[![Enigma machine typewriter keypad over many rotors in a wood box](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Enigma_Machine_at_NSA.jpg/250px-Enigma_Machine_at_NSA.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Enigma_Machine_at_NSA.jpg)

Variants of the [Enigma machine](/wiki/Enigma_machine "Enigma machine"), used by Germany's military and civil authorities from the late 1920s through [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), implemented a complex electro-mechanical polyalphabetic [cipher](/wiki/Cipher "Cipher"). [Breaking and reading of the Enigma cipher](/wiki/Cryptanalysis_of_the_Enigma "Cryptanalysis of the Enigma") at Poland's [Cipher Bureau](/wiki/Biuro_Szyfr%C3%B3w "Biuro Szyfrów"), for 7 years before the war, and subsequent decryption at [Bletchley Park](/wiki/Bletchley_Park "Bletchley Park"), was important to Allied victory.[[13]](#cite_note-Kahn-1967-13)

The goal of cryptanalysis is to find some weakness or insecurity in a cryptographic scheme, thus permitting its subversion or evasion.

It is a common misconception that every encryption method can be broken. In connection with his WWII work at [Bell Labs](/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs"), [Claude Shannon](/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon") proved that the [one-time pad](/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad") cipher is unbreakable, provided the key material is truly [random](/wiki/Statistical_randomness "Statistical randomness"), never reused, kept secret from all possible attackers, and of equal or greater length than the message.[[63]](#cite_note-63) Most [ciphers](/wiki/Cipher "Cipher"), apart from the one-time pad, can be broken with enough computational effort by [brute force attack](/wiki/Brute_force_attack "Brute force attack"), but the amount of effort needed may be [exponentially](/wiki/Exponential_time "Exponential time") dependent on the key size, as compared to the effort needed to make use of the cipher. In such cases, effective security could be achieved if it is proven that the effort required (i.e., "work factor", in Shannon's terms) is beyond the ability of any adversary. This means it must be shown that no efficient method (as opposed to the time-consuming brute force method) can be found to break the cipher. Since no such proof has been found to date, the one-time-pad remains the only theoretically unbreakable cipher. Although well-implemented one-time-pad encryption cannot be broken, traffic analysis is still possible.

There are a wide variety of cryptanalytic attacks, and they can be classified in any of several ways. A common distinction turns on what Eve (an attacker) knows and what capabilities are available. In a [ciphertext-only attack](/wiki/Ciphertext-only_attack "Ciphertext-only attack"), Eve has access only to the ciphertext (good modern cryptosystems are usually effectively immune to ciphertext-only attacks). In a [known-plaintext attack](/wiki/Known-plaintext_attack "Known-plaintext attack"), Eve has access to a ciphertext and its corresponding plaintext (or to many such pairs). In a [chosen-plaintext attack](/wiki/Chosen-plaintext_attack "Chosen-plaintext attack"), Eve may choose a plaintext and learn its corresponding ciphertext (perhaps many times); an example is [gardening](/wiki/Gardening_(cryptanalysis) "Gardening (cryptanalysis)"), used by the British during WWII. In a [chosen-ciphertext attack](/wiki/Chosen-ciphertext_attack "Chosen-ciphertext attack"), Eve may be able to *choose* ciphertexts and learn their corresponding plaintexts.[[5]](#cite_note-Menezes-1997-5) Finally in a [man-in-the-middle](/wiki/Man-in-the-middle_attack "Man-in-the-middle attack") attack Eve gets in between Alice (the sender) and Bob (the recipient), accesses and modifies the traffic and then forward it to the recipient.[[64]](#cite_note-64) Also important, often overwhelmingly so, are mistakes (generally in the design or use of one of the [protocols](/wiki/Cryptographic_protocol "Cryptographic protocol") involved).

Cryptanalysis of symmetric-key ciphers typically involves looking for attacks against the block ciphers or stream ciphers that are more efficient than any attack that could be against a perfect cipher. For example, a simple brute force attack against DES requires one known plaintext and 255 decryptions, trying approximately half of the possible keys, to reach a point at which chances are better than even that the key sought will have been found. But this may not be enough assurance; a [linear cryptanalysis](/wiki/Linear_cryptanalysis "Linear cryptanalysis") attack against DES requires 243 known plaintexts (with their corresponding ciphertexts) and approximately 243 DES operations.[[65]](#cite_note-65) This is a considerable improvement over brute force attacks.

Public-key algorithms are based on the computational difficulty of various problems. The most famous of these are the difficulty of [integer factorization](/wiki/Integer_factorization "Integer factorization") of [semiprimes](/wiki/Semiprime "Semiprime") and the difficulty of calculating [discrete logarithms](/wiki/Discrete_logarithm "Discrete logarithm"), both of which are not yet proven to be solvable in [polynomial time](/wiki/P_(complexity) "P (complexity)") (**P**) using only a classical [Turing-complete](/wiki/Turing_completeness "Turing completeness") computer. Much public-key cryptanalysis concerns designing algorithms in **P** that can solve these problems, or using other technologies, such as [quantum computers](/wiki/Quantum_computing "Quantum computing"). For instance, the best-known algorithms for solving the [elliptic curve-based](/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography "Elliptic curve cryptography") version of discrete logarithm are much more time-consuming than the best-known algorithms for factoring, at least for problems of more or less equivalent size. Thus, to achieve an equivalent strength of encryption, techniques that depend upon the difficulty of factoring large composite numbers, such as the RSA cryptosystem, require larger keys than elliptic curve techniques. For this reason, public-key cryptosystems based on elliptic curves have become popular since their invention in the mid-1990s.

While pure cryptanalysis uses weaknesses in the algorithms themselves, other attacks on cryptosystems are based on actual use of the algorithms in real devices, and are called *[side-channel attacks](/wiki/Side-channel_attack "Side-channel attack")*. If a cryptanalyst has access to, for example, the amount of time the device took to encrypt a number of plaintexts or report an error in a password or PIN character, they may be able to use a [timing attack](/wiki/Timing_attack "Timing attack") to break a cipher that is otherwise resistant to analysis. An attacker might also study the pattern and length of messages to derive valuable information; this is known as [traffic analysis](/wiki/Traffic_analysis "Traffic analysis")[[66]](#cite_note-66) and can be quite useful to an alert adversary. Poor administration of a cryptosystem, such as permitting too short keys, will make any system vulnerable, regardless of other virtues. [Social engineering](/wiki/Social_engineering_(security) "Social engineering (security)") and other attacks against humans (e.g., bribery, [extortion](/wiki/Extortion "Extortion"), [blackmail](/wiki/Blackmail "Blackmail"), espionage, [rubber-hose cryptanalysis](/wiki/Rubber-hose_cryptanalysis "Rubber-hose cryptanalysis") or torture) are usually employed due to being more cost-effective and feasible to perform in a reasonable amount of time compared to pure cryptanalysis by a high margin.

### Cryptographic primitives

Much of the theoretical work in cryptography concerns [cryptographic *primitives*](/wiki/Cryptographic_primitive "Cryptographic primitive")—algorithms with basic cryptographic properties—and their relationship to other cryptographic problems. More complicated cryptographic tools are then built from these basic primitives. These primitives provide fundamental properties, which are used to develop more complex tools called *cryptosystems* or *cryptographic protocols*, which guarantee one or more high-level security properties. Note, however, that the distinction between cryptographic *primitives* and cryptosystems, is quite arbitrary; for example, the RSA algorithm is sometimes considered a cryptosystem, and sometimes a primitive. Typical examples of cryptographic primitives include [pseudorandom functions](/wiki/Pseudorandom_function "Pseudorandom function"), [one-way functions](/wiki/One-way_function "One-way function"), etc.

### Cryptosystems

Main article: [List of cryptosystems](/wiki/List_of_cryptosystems "List of cryptosystems")

One or more cryptographic primitives are often used to develop a more complex algorithm, called a cryptographic system, or *cryptosystem*. Cryptosystems (e.g., [El-Gamal encryption](/wiki/ElGamal_encryption "ElGamal encryption")) are designed to provide particular functionality (e.g., public key encryption) while guaranteeing certain security properties (e.g., [chosen-plaintext attack (CPA)](/wiki/Chosen-plaintext_attack "Chosen-plaintext attack") security in the [random oracle model](/wiki/Random_oracle_model "Random oracle model")). Cryptosystems use the properties of the underlying cryptographic primitives to support the system's security properties. As the distinction between primitives and cryptosystems is somewhat arbitrary, a sophisticated cryptosystem can be derived from a combination of several more primitive cryptosystems. In many cases, the cryptosystem's structure involves back and forth communication among two or more parties in space (e.g., between the sender of a secure message and its receiver) or across time (e.g., cryptographically protected [backup](/wiki/Backup "Backup") data). Such cryptosystems are sometimes called *[cryptographic protocols](/wiki/Cryptographic_protocol "Cryptographic protocol")*.

Some widely known cryptosystems include RSA, [Schnorr signature](/wiki/Schnorr_signature "Schnorr signature"), [ElGamal encryption](/wiki/ElGamal_encryption "ElGamal encryption"), and [Pretty Good Privacy](/wiki/Pretty_Good_Privacy "Pretty Good Privacy") (PGP). More complex cryptosystems include [electronic cash](/wiki/Digital_currency "Digital currency")[[67]](#cite_note-67) systems, [signcryption](/wiki/Signcryption "Signcryption") systems, etc. Some more 'theoretical'[*[clarification needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")*] cryptosystems include [interactive proof systems](/wiki/Interactive_proof_system "Interactive proof system"),[[68]](#cite_note-68) (like [zero-knowledge proofs](/wiki/Zero-knowledge_proof "Zero-knowledge proof"))[[69]](#cite_note-69) and systems for [secret sharing](/wiki/Secret_sharing "Secret sharing").[[70]](#cite_note-70)[[71]](#cite_note-71)

### Lightweight cryptography

Lightweight cryptography (LWC) concerns cryptographic algorithms developed for a strictly constrained environment. The growth of [Internet of Things (IoT)](/wiki/Internet_of_things "Internet of things") has spiked research into the development of lightweight algorithms that are better suited for the environment. An IoT environment requires strict constraints on power consumption, processing power, and security.[[72]](#cite_note-72) Algorithms such as [Ascon](/wiki/Ascon_(cipher) "Ascon (cipher)") and [SPECK](/wiki/Speck_(cipher) "Speck (cipher)") are examples of the many LWC algorithms that have been developed to match the criteria for the [CAESAR Competition](/wiki/CAESAR_Competition "CAESAR Competition") and the standard set by the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology "National Institute of Standards and Technology").[[73]](#cite_note-73)

## Applications

Cryptography is widely used on the internet to help protect user-data and prevent eavesdropping. To ensure secrecy during transmission, many systems use private key cryptography to protect transmitted information. With public-key systems, one can maintain secrecy without a master key or a large number of keys.[[74]](#cite_note-74) But, some algorithms like [BitLocker](/wiki/BitLocker "BitLocker") and [VeraCrypt](/wiki/VeraCrypt "VeraCrypt") are generally not private-public key cryptography. For example, Veracrypt uses a password hash to generate the single private key. However, it can be configured to run in public-private key systems. The [C++](/wiki/C%2B%2B "C++") opensource encryption library [OpenSSL](/wiki/OpenSSL "OpenSSL") provides [free and opensource](/wiki/Free_and_open-source_software "Free and open-source software") encryption software and tools. The most commonly used encryption cipher suit is [AES](/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard "Advanced Encryption Standard"),[[75]](#cite_note-75) as it has hardware acceleration for all [x86](/wiki/X86 "X86") based processors that has [AES-NI](/wiki/AES_instruction_set "AES instruction set"). A close contender is [ChaCha20-Poly1305](/wiki/ChaCha20-Poly1305 "ChaCha20-Poly1305"), which is a [stream cipher](/wiki/Stream_cipher "Stream cipher"), however it is commonly used for mobile devices as they are [ARM](/wiki/ARM_architecture_family "ARM architecture family") based which does not feature AES-NI instruction set extension.

### Cybersecurity

Main article: [Cybersecurity](/wiki/Cybersecurity "Cybersecurity")

Cryptography can be used to secure communications by encrypting them. Websites use encryption via [HTTPS](/wiki/HTTPS "HTTPS").[[76]](#cite_note-Chamberlain-2017-76) "End-to-end" encryption, where only sender and receiver can read messages, is implemented for email in [Pretty Good Privacy](/wiki/Pretty_Good_Privacy "Pretty Good Privacy") and for secure messaging in general in [WhatsApp](/wiki/WhatsApp "WhatsApp"), [Signal](/wiki/Signal_(messaging_app) "Signal (messaging app)") and [Telegram](/wiki/Telegram_(software) "Telegram (software)").[[76]](#cite_note-Chamberlain-2017-76)

Operating systems use encryption to keep passwords secret, conceal parts of the system, and ensure that software updates are truly from the system maker.[[76]](#cite_note-Chamberlain-2017-76) Instead of storing plaintext passwords, computer systems store hashes thereof; then, when a user logs in, the system passes the given password through a cryptographic hash function and compares it to the hashed value on file. In this manner, neither the system nor an attacker has at any point access to the password in plaintext.[[76]](#cite_note-Chamberlain-2017-76)

Encryption is sometimes used to encrypt one's entire drive. For example, [University College London](/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") has implemented [BitLocker](/wiki/BitLocker "BitLocker") (a program by Microsoft) to render drive data opaque without users logging in.[[76]](#cite_note-Chamberlain-2017-76)

### Cryptocurrencies and cryptoeconomics

Cryptographic techniques enable [cryptocurrency](/wiki/Cryptocurrency "Cryptocurrency") technologies, such as [distributed ledger technologies](/wiki/Distributed_Ledger_Technology "Distributed Ledger Technology") (e.g., [blockchains](/wiki/Blockchain "Blockchain")), which finance [cryptoeconomics](/wiki/Cryptoeconomics "Cryptoeconomics") applications such as [decentralized finance (DeFi)](/wiki/Decentralized_finance "Decentralized finance"). Key cryptographic techniques that enable cryptocurrencies and cryptoeconomics include, but are not limited to: [cryptographic keys](/wiki/Cryptographic_keys "Cryptographic keys"), cryptographic hash function, [asymmetric (public key) encryption](/wiki/Public_key_encryption "Public key encryption"), [Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)](/wiki/Multi-factor_authentication "Multi-factor authentication"), [End-to-End Encryption (E2EE)](/wiki/End-to-end_encryption "End-to-end encryption"), and [Zero Knowledge Proofs (ZKP)](/wiki/Zero-knowledge_proof "Zero-knowledge proof").

### Quantum computing cybersecurity

Estimates suggest that a [quantum computer](/wiki/Quantum_computer "Quantum computer") could reduce the effort required to break today's strongest [RSA](/wiki/RSA_(cryptosystem) "RSA (cryptosystem)") or [elliptic-curve](/wiki/Elliptic-curve_cryptography "Elliptic-curve cryptography") keys from millennia to mere seconds, rendering current protocols (such as the versions of [TLS](/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security "Transport Layer Security") that rely on those keys) insecure.[[77]](#cite_note-77)

To mitigate this "quantum threat", researchers are developing [quantum-resistant algorithms](/wiki/Post-quantum_cryptography "Post-quantum cryptography") whose security rests on problems believed to remain hard for both classical and quantum computers.[[78]](#cite_note-78)

## Legal issues

See also: [Cryptography laws in different nations](/wiki/Cryptography_laws_in_different_nations "Cryptography laws in different nations")

### Prohibitions

Cryptography has long been of interest to intelligence gathering and [law enforcement agencies](/wiki/Law_enforcement_agency "Law enforcement agency").[[9]](#cite_note-Ranger-2015-9) Secret communications may be criminal or even [treasonous](/wiki/Treason "Treason").[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*] Because of its facilitation of [privacy](/wiki/Privacy "Privacy"), and the diminution of privacy attendant on its prohibition, cryptography is also of considerable interest to civil rights supporters. Accordingly, there has been a history of controversial legal issues surrounding cryptography, especially since the advent of inexpensive computers has made widespread access to high-quality cryptography possible.

In some countries, even the domestic use of cryptography is, or has been, restricted. Until 1999, [France](/wiki/France "France") significantly restricted the use of cryptography domestically, though it has since relaxed many of these rules. In [China](/wiki/China "China") and [Iran](/wiki/Islamic_Republic_of_Iran "Islamic Republic of Iran"), a license is still required to use cryptography.[[7]](#cite_note-Crypto_Law_Survey-2013-7) Many countries have tight restrictions on the use of cryptography. Among the more restrictive are laws in [Belarus](/wiki/Belarus "Belarus"), [Kazakhstan](/wiki/Kazakhstan "Kazakhstan"), [Mongolia](/wiki/Mongolia "Mongolia"), [Pakistan](/wiki/Pakistan "Pakistan"), [Singapore](/wiki/Singapore "Singapore"), [Tunisia](/wiki/Tunisia "Tunisia"), and [Vietnam](/wiki/Vietnam "Vietnam").[[79]](#cite_note-79)

In the United States, cryptography is legal for domestic use, but there has been much conflict over legal issues related to cryptography.[[9]](#cite_note-Ranger-2015-9) One particularly important issue has been the [export of cryptography](/wiki/Export_of_cryptography "Export of cryptography") and cryptographic software and hardware. Probably because of the importance of cryptanalysis in [World War II](/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") and an expectation that cryptography would continue to be important for national security, many Western governments have, at some point, strictly regulated export of cryptography. After World War II, it was illegal in the US to sell or distribute encryption technology overseas; in fact, encryption was designated as auxiliary military equipment and put on the [United States Munitions List](/wiki/United_States_Munitions_List "United States Munitions List").[[80]](#cite_note-80) Until the development of the personal computer, asymmetric key algorithms (i.e., public key techniques), and the Internet, this was not especially problematic. However, as the Internet grew and computers became more widely available, high-quality encryption techniques became well known around the globe.

### Export controls

Main article: [Export of cryptography](/wiki/Export_of_cryptography "Export of cryptography")

In the 1990s, there were several challenges to US export regulation of cryptography. After the [source code](/wiki/Source_code "Source code") for [Philip Zimmermann](/wiki/Philip_Zimmermann "Philip Zimmermann")'s [Pretty Good Privacy](/wiki/Pretty_Good_Privacy "Pretty Good Privacy") (PGP) encryption program found its way onto the Internet in June 1991, a complaint by [RSA Security](/wiki/RSA_Security "RSA Security") (then called RSA Data Security, Inc.) resulted in a lengthy criminal investigation of Zimmermann by the US Customs Service and the [FBI](/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation "Federal Bureau of Investigation"), though no charges were ever filed.[[81]](#cite_note-81)[[82]](#cite_note-Levy-2001-82) [Daniel J. Bernstein](/wiki/Daniel_J._Bernstein "Daniel J. Bernstein"), then a graduate student at [UC Berkeley](/wiki/UC_Berkeley "UC Berkeley"), brought a lawsuit against the US government challenging some aspects of the restrictions based on [free speech](/wiki/First_Amendment "First Amendment") grounds. The 1995 case *[Bernstein v. United States](/wiki/Bernstein_v._United_States "Bernstein v. United States")* ultimately resulted in a 1999 decision that printed source code for cryptographic algorithms and systems was protected as [free speech](/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech") by the United States Constitution.[[83]](#cite_note-83)

In 1996, thirty-nine countries signed the [Wassenaar Arrangement](/wiki/Wassenaar_Arrangement "Wassenaar Arrangement"), an arms control treaty that deals with the export of arms and "dual-use" technologies such as cryptography. The treaty stipulated that the use of cryptography with short key-lengths (56-bit for symmetric encryption, 512-bit for RSA) would no longer be export-controlled.[[84]](#cite_note-84) Cryptography exports from the US became less strictly regulated as a consequence of a major relaxation in 2000;[[85]](#cite_note-85) there are no longer very many restrictions on key sizes in US-[exported](/wiki/Export_of_cryptography "Export of cryptography") mass-market software. Since this relaxation in US export restrictions, and because most personal computers connected to the Internet include US-sourced [web browsers](/wiki/Web_browser "Web browser") such as [Firefox](/wiki/Firefox "Firefox") or [Internet Explorer](/wiki/Internet_Explorer "Internet Explorer"), almost every Internet user worldwide has potential access to quality cryptography via their browsers (e.g., via [Transport Layer Security](/wiki/Transport_Layer_Security "Transport Layer Security")). The [Mozilla Thunderbird](/wiki/Mozilla_Thunderbird "Mozilla Thunderbird") and [Microsoft Outlook](/wiki/Microsoft_Outlook "Microsoft Outlook") [E-mail client](/wiki/E-mail_client "E-mail client") programs similarly can transmit and receive emails via TLS, and can send and receive email encrypted with [S/MIME](/wiki/S/MIME "S/MIME"). Many Internet users do not realize that their basic application software contains such extensive [cryptosystems](/wiki/Cryptosystem "Cryptosystem"). These browsers and email programs are so ubiquitous that even governments whose intent is to regulate civilian use of cryptography generally do not find it practical to do much to control distribution or use of cryptography of this quality, so even when such laws are in force, actual enforcement is often effectively impossible.[*[citation needed](/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### NSA involvement

See also: [Clipper chip](/wiki/Clipper_chip "Clipper chip")

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/84/National_Security_Agency_headquarters%2C_Fort_Meade%2C_Maryland.jpg/250px-National_Security_Agency_headquarters%2C_Fort_Meade%2C_Maryland.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:National_Security_Agency_headquarters,_Fort_Meade,_Maryland.jpg)

NSA headquarters in Fort Meade, Maryland

Another contentious issue connected to cryptography in the United States is the influence of the [National Security Agency](/wiki/National_Security_Agency "National Security Agency") on cipher development and policy.[[9]](#cite_note-Ranger-2015-9) The NSA was involved with the design of [DES](/wiki/Data_Encryption_Standard "Data Encryption Standard") during its development at [IBM](/wiki/IBM "IBM") and its consideration by the [National Bureau of Standards](/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards "National Bureau of Standards") as a possible Federal Standard for cryptography.[[86]](#cite_note-86) DES was designed to be resistant to [differential cryptanalysis](/wiki/Differential_cryptanalysis "Differential cryptanalysis"),[[87]](#cite_note-87) a powerful and general cryptanalytic technique known to the NSA and IBM, that became publicly known only when it was rediscovered in the late 1980s.[[88]](#cite_note-88) According to [Steven Levy](/wiki/Steven_Levy "Steven Levy"), IBM discovered differential cryptanalysis,[[82]](#cite_note-Levy-2001-82) but kept the technique secret at the NSA's request. The technique became publicly known only when Biham and Shamir re-discovered and announced it some years later. The entire affair illustrates the difficulty of determining what resources and knowledge an attacker might actually have.

Another instance of the NSA's involvement was the 1993 [Clipper chip](/wiki/Clipper_chip "Clipper chip") affair, an encryption microchip intended to be part of the [Capstone](/wiki/Capstone_(cryptography) "Capstone (cryptography)") cryptography-control initiative. Clipper was widely criticized by cryptographers for two reasons. The cipher algorithm (called [Skipjack](/wiki/Skipjack_(cipher) "Skipjack (cipher)")) was then classified (declassified in 1998, long after the Clipper initiative lapsed). The classified cipher caused concerns that the NSA had deliberately made the cipher weak to assist its intelligence efforts. The whole initiative was also criticized based on its violation of [Kerckhoffs's Principle](/wiki/Kerckhoffs%27s_Principle "Kerckhoffs's Principle"), as the scheme included a special [escrow key](/wiki/Key_escrow "Key escrow") held by the government for use by law enforcement (i.e. [wiretapping](/wiki/Telephone_tapping "Telephone tapping")).[[82]](#cite_note-Levy-2001-82)

### Digital rights management

Main article: [Digital rights management](/wiki/Digital_rights_management "Digital rights management")

Cryptography is central to digital rights management (DRM), a group of techniques for technologically controlling use of [copyrighted](/wiki/Copyright "Copyright") material, being widely implemented and deployed at the behest of some copyright holders. In 1998, U.S. President [Bill Clinton](/wiki/Bill_Clinton "Bill Clinton") signed the [Digital Millennium Copyright Act](/wiki/Digital_Millennium_Copyright_Act "Digital Millennium Copyright Act") (DMCA), which criminalized all production, dissemination, and use of certain cryptanalytic techniques and technology (now known or later discovered); specifically, those that could be used to circumvent DRM technological schemes.[[89]](#cite_note-89) This had a noticeable impact on the cryptography research community since an argument can be made that any cryptanalytic research violated the DMCA. Similar statutes have since been enacted in several countries and regions, including the implementation in the [EU Copyright Directive](/wiki/Directive_on_the_harmonisation_of_certain_aspects_of_copyright_and_related_rights_in_the_information_society "Directive on the harmonisation of certain aspects of copyright and related rights in the information society"). Similar restrictions are called for by treaties signed by [World Intellectual Property Organization](/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Organization "World Intellectual Property Organization") member-states.

The [United States Department of Justice](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice "United States Department of Justice") and [FBI](/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation "Federal Bureau of Investigation") have not enforced the DMCA as rigorously as had been feared by some, but the law, nonetheless, remains a controversial one. [Niels Ferguson](/wiki/Niels_Ferguson "Niels Ferguson"), a well-respected cryptography researcher, has publicly stated that he will not release some of his research into an [Intel](/wiki/Intel_Corporation "Intel Corporation") security design for fear of prosecution under the DMCA.[[90]](#cite_note-90) Cryptologist [Bruce Schneier](/wiki/Bruce_Schneier "Bruce Schneier") has argued that the DMCA encourages [vendor lock-in](/wiki/Vendor_lock-in "Vendor lock-in"), while inhibiting actual measures toward cyber-security.[[91]](#cite_note-91) Both [Alan Cox](/wiki/Alan_Cox_(computer_programmer) "Alan Cox (computer programmer)") (longtime [Linux kernel](/wiki/Linux_kernel "Linux kernel") developer) and [Edward Felten](/wiki/Edward_Felten "Edward Felten") (and some of his students at Princeton) have encountered problems related to the Act. [Dmitry Sklyarov](/wiki/Dmitry_Sklyarov "Dmitry Sklyarov") was arrested during a visit to the US from Russia, and jailed for five months pending trial for alleged violations of the DMCA arising from work he had done in Russia, where the work was legal. In 2007, the cryptographic keys responsible for [Blu-ray](/wiki/Blu-ray "Blu-ray") and [HD DVD](/wiki/HD_DVD "HD DVD") content scrambling were [discovered and released onto the Internet](/wiki/AACS_encryption_key_controversy "AACS encryption key controversy"). In both cases, the [Motion Picture Association of America](/wiki/MPAA "MPAA") sent out numerous DMCA takedown notices, and there was a massive Internet backlash[[10]](#cite_note-Doctorow-2007-10) triggered by the perceived impact of such notices on [fair use](/wiki/Fair_use "Fair use") and [free speech](/wiki/Free_speech "Free speech").

### Forced disclosure of encryption keys

Main article: [Key disclosure law](/wiki/Key_disclosure_law "Key disclosure law")

In the United Kingdom, the [Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act](/wiki/Regulation_of_Investigatory_Powers_Act_2000 "Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000") gives UK police the powers to force suspects to decrypt files or hand over passwords that protect encryption keys. Failure to comply is an offense in its own right, punishable on conviction by a two-year jail sentence or up to five years in cases involving national security.[[8]](#cite_note-PC_World-2007-8) Successful prosecutions have occurred under the Act; the first, in 2009,[[92]](#cite_note-92) resulted in a term of 13 months' imprisonment.[[93]](#cite_note-93) Similar forced disclosure laws in Australia, Finland, France, and India compel individual suspects under investigation to hand over encryption keys or passwords during a criminal investigation.

In the United States, the federal criminal case of *[United States v. Fricosu](/wiki/United_States_v._Fricosu "United States v. Fricosu")* addressed whether a search warrant can compel a person to reveal an [encryption](/wiki/Encryption "Encryption") [passphrase](/wiki/Passphrase "Passphrase") or password.[[94]](#cite_note-94) The [Electronic Frontier Foundation](/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation "Electronic Frontier Foundation") (EFF) argued that this is a violation of the protection from self-incrimination given by the [Fifth Amendment](/wiki/Fifth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution "Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution").[[95]](#cite_note-95) In 2012, the court ruled that under the [All Writs Act](/wiki/All_Writs_Act "All Writs Act"), the defendant was required to produce an unencrypted hard drive for the court.[[96]](#cite_note-96)

In many jurisdictions, the legal status of forced disclosure remains unclear.

The 2016 [FBI–Apple encryption dispute](/wiki/FBI%E2%80%93Apple_encryption_dispute "FBI–Apple encryption dispute") concerns the ability of courts in the United States to compel manufacturers' assistance in unlocking cell phones whose contents are cryptographically protected.

As a potential counter-measure to forced disclosure some cryptographic software supports [plausible deniability](/wiki/Plausible_deniability "Plausible deniability"), where the encrypted data is indistinguishable from unused random data (for example such as that of a [drive which has been securely wiped](/wiki/Data_remanence "Data remanence")).
