Specialized data centers designed for artificial intelligence

An **AI data center** is a specialized [data center](/wiki/Data_center "Data center") facility designed for the computationally intensive tasks of training and running [artificial intelligence](/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") (AI) and [machine learning](/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") models. Unlike general-purpose data centers, they are often optimized for the parallel processing demands of AI workloads, typically using hardware such as [AI accelerators](/wiki/AI_accelerator "AI accelerator") ([GPUs](/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit "Graphics processing unit"), [TPUs](/wiki/Tensor_Processing_Unit "Tensor Processing Unit")) and high-speed [interconnects](/wiki/Interconnect_(integrated_circuits) "Interconnect (integrated circuits)").

The global push to construct these specialized facilities accelerated dramatically during the [AI boom](/wiki/AI_boom "AI boom") of the 2020s. In 2026, major tech companies were estimated to spend $650 billion on AI data centers.[[1]](#cite_note-1)[[2]](#cite_note-2) Their purchases of [memory chips](/wiki/Semiconductor_memory "Semiconductor memory"), particularly [High Bandwidth Memory](/wiki/High_Bandwidth_Memory "High Bandwidth Memory"), led to a [global memory supply shortage](/wiki/2024%E2%80%932026_global_memory_supply_shortage "2024–2026 global memory supply shortage") amid a broader competition for semiconductors, power, and infrastructure.[[3]](#cite_note-reuters-ai-frenzy-3)[[4]](#cite_note-4)[[5]](#cite_note-5)[[6]](#cite_note-6) About 70% of global [computer memory](/wiki/Computer_memory "Computer memory") production in [fiscal year](/wiki/Fiscal_year "Fiscal year") 2026 has been purchased for AI data centers.[[7]](#cite_note-7)

AI-focused data centers use around 60 kilowatts of electricity per [server rack](/wiki/Server_rack "Server rack"), while general-purpose data centers typically use around 10 kilowatts of electricity per rack.[[8]](#cite_note-8)[[9]](#cite_note-Leppert-9)[[10]](#cite_note-10)

By 2026, many U.S. communities had organized against the local construction of AI data centers, which can consume large amounts of power and water and generate heat, noise, and other pollutants. By July 2026, local community resistance had blocked the construction of AI data centers worth some $130 billion.[[11]](#cite_note-11)

## Operators

As of August 2025, [The Information](/wiki/The_Information_(website) "The Information (website)") tracked 18 planned or existing AI data centers in the United States, operated by [Amazon Web Services](/wiki/Amazon_Web_Services "Amazon Web Services"), [CoreWeave](/wiki/CoreWeave "CoreWeave"), Crusoe, [Meta](/wiki/Meta_Platforms "Meta Platforms"), [Microsoft](/wiki/Microsoft "Microsoft")/[OpenAI](/wiki/OpenAI "OpenAI"), [Oracle](/wiki/Oracle_Corporation "Oracle Corporation"), [Tesla](/wiki/Tesla,_Inc. "Tesla, Inc."), and [xAI](/wiki/XAI_(company) "XAI (company)").[[12]](#cite_note-12) Other AI data center operators include [Digital Realty](/wiki/Digital_Realty "Digital Realty") and [Alibaba](/wiki/Alibaba_Group "Alibaba Group").[[13]](#cite_note-Booth-13) Data centers are also being built in China, India, Europe,[[13]](#cite_note-Booth-13) Saudi Arabia,[[14]](#cite_note-14) and Canada.[[15]](#cite_note-15) *[The New Yorker](/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker")* described CoreWeave as the most prominent AI data center operator in the United States.[[16]](#cite_note-16)

Two types of data center providers for machine learning have been noted in [news media](/wiki/News_media "News media"): hyperscalers and neoclouds. *[The Verge](/wiki/The_Verge "The Verge")* listed large technology companies such as Google, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and Amazon as hyperscalers.[[17]](#cite_note-Lopatto-17) *[The New York Times](/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* described neoclouds as "a new generation of data center providers". CoreWeave, Nebius, [Nscale](/wiki/NScale "NScale"), and Lambda have been described as examples of neoclouds.[[18]](#cite_note-Weise-18)

In January 2025, OpenAI, in partnership with Oracle and [Softbank](/wiki/SoftBank_Group "SoftBank Group"), announced the [Stargate](/wiki/Stargate_LLC "Stargate LLC") project, which as of September 2025 is composed of six built or proposed AI data centers in the United States.[[19]](#cite_note-19)

In response to the Stargate project, [Amazon](/wiki/Amazon_(company) "Amazon (company)") launched in October 2025 an AI data center on 1,200 acres of farmland in Indiana. This data center, known as Project Rainier, is one of the largest AI data centers in the world, with Amazon spending $11 billion on the project. Rainier is specifically intended for training and running machine learning models from [Anthropic](/wiki/Anthropic "Anthropic").[[20]](#cite_note-20) As of that time, this facility contains seven data centers (out of an estimated 30 planned) and will use 2.2 gigawatts of electricity (equivalent to 1 million households) and millions of gallons of water per year. Computer chips from [Annapurna Labs](/wiki/Annapurna_Labs "Annapurna Labs") and Anthropic, Trainium 2, were designed for use in such facilities. Amazon pumped millions of gallons of water out of the ground to construct the data center, and as of June 2025, Indiana state officials are investigating whether this [dewatering](/wiki/Dewatering "Dewatering") process led to dry wells for local residents.[[21]](#cite_note-21)

In November 2025, Anthropic announced a partnership with Fluidstack to develop [artificial intelligence infrastructure](/wiki/AI_infrastructure "AI infrastructure") in the United States, including data centers in New York and Texas, worth $50 billion.[[22]](#cite_note-22)

Other AI data center projects include the [Colossus supercomputer](/wiki/Colossus_(supercomputer) "Colossus (supercomputer)") from xAI, a Louisiana-based project from Meta, Hyperion, expected to use 5 GW of power, and a second Ohio-based Meta project, Prometheus, with a capacity of 1 GW.[[23]](#cite_note-23) A 3,200-acre AI data center, capable of 4.4-4.5 GW of power and located on the decommissioned [Homer City Generating Station](/wiki/Homer_City_Generating_Station "Homer City Generating Station"), is under construction as of 2025, and will use seven 30-acre gas generating stations supplied by [EQT](/wiki/EQT_Corporation "EQT Corporation").[[24]](#cite_note-Halper-24)[[25]](#cite_note-25)

As of December 2025, [CRH](/wiki/CRH_plc "CRH plc") is working on over 100 data centers in the United States.[[26]](#cite_note-Berg-26)

In 2025, [ExxonMobil](/wiki/ExxonMobil "ExxonMobil") and [NextEra](/wiki/NextEra_Energy "NextEra Energy") announced plans to build a data center powered by [natural gas](/wiki/Natural_gas "Natural gas") and using [carbon capture technology](/wiki/Carbon_capture_and_storage "Carbon capture and storage"), with 1.2 GW of power capacity. They previously purchased 2,500 acres of land in the [Southeastern United States](/wiki/Southeastern_United_States "Southeastern United States") and plan to market the data center to an artificial intelligence company.[[27]](#cite_note-27)

The increased interest in AI data centers has led to several executives from companies in that space becoming billionaires, including CoreWeave, [QTS](/wiki/Quality_Technology_Services "Quality Technology Services"), [Nebius](/wiki/Nebius_Group "Nebius Group"), [Astera Labs](/wiki/Astera_Labs "Astera Labs"), [Groq](/wiki/Groq "Groq"), Fermi (which is connected to former United States Secretary of Energy [Rick Perry](/wiki/Rick_Perry "Rick Perry")), [Snowflake](/wiki/Snowflake_Inc. "Snowflake Inc.") and Cipher Mining.[[28]](#cite_note-28)

Several companies involved in [cryptocurrency mining](/wiki/Cryptocurrency_mining "Cryptocurrency mining"), such as [Bitdeer](/wiki/Bitdeer "Bitdeer"), CoreWeave,[[29]](#cite_note-Nicoletti-29) Cipher Mining, [TeraWulf](/wiki/TeraWulf "TeraWulf"),[[30]](#cite_note-30) IREN, Core Scientific, and CleanSpark[[31]](#cite_note-31) have also been involved with AI data centers.

## Finances

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Private_investment_in_generative_AI_-_2024_AI_index.jpg/250px-Private_investment_in_generative_AI_-_2024_AI_index.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Private_investment_in_generative_AI_-_2024_AI_index.jpg)

Between January and August 2024, Microsoft, Meta, Google and Amazon collectively spent $125 billion on AI data centers.[[32]](#cite_note-32) [Citigroup](/wiki/Citigroup "Citigroup") forecasted that $2.8 trillion would be spent on AI data centers by 2030,[[13]](#cite_note-Booth-13) while [McKinsey and Company](/wiki/McKinsey_and_Company "McKinsey and Company") estimated that almost $7 trillion would be spent globally by that time.[[33]](#cite_note-Kerr-33) According to [S&P Global](/wiki/S%26P_Global "S&P Global"), $61 billion has been spent on the data center market as a whole in 2025, while debt issuance for data centers was $182 billion during the same year.[[34]](#cite_note-34)

Large technology companies have offloaded the financial risks of building AI data centers by setting up [special purpose vehicles](/wiki/Special-purpose_entity "Special-purpose entity") or by contracting with neoclouds. For example, Meta's Hyperion was mostly funded by [Blue Owl Capital](/wiki/Blue_Owl_Capital "Blue Owl Capital"), which did so using a bond offering from [PIMCO](/wiki/PIMCO "PIMCO"). Those bonds were sold to a number of clients, including [BlackRock](/wiki/BlackRock "BlackRock"). Meta did not borrow money itself and instead established a special purpose vehicle from which it would rent the data center.[[18]](#cite_note-Weise-18) This deal was structured by [Morgan Stanley](/wiki/Morgan_Stanley "Morgan Stanley") for $30 billion, the largest known private capital transaction as of 2025.[[29]](#cite_note-Nicoletti-29)

Neoclouds such as CoreWeave have gone into debt to buy computer chips from Nvidia for their data centers, and the chips themselves have been used for loan collateral. As of December 2025, CoreWeave took out three GPU-backed loans, collectively worth $12.4 billion, from private credit firms ([Blackstone](/wiki/Blackstone_Inc. "Blackstone Inc."), [Coatue](/wiki/Coatue_Management "Coatue Management"), [BlackRock](/wiki/BlackRock "BlackRock"), [PIMCO](/wiki/PIMCO "PIMCO")) and from banks ([Goldman Sachs](/wiki/Goldman_Sachs "Goldman Sachs"), [JPMorgan Chase](/wiki/JPMorgan_Chase "JPMorgan Chase"), [Wells Fargo](/wiki/Wells_Fargo "Wells Fargo")). Thus, these companies provide an indirect connection between private credit and established banks. Data centers have also established [asset-backed securities](/wiki/Asset-backed_securities "Asset-backed securities"), and debt for data centers has its own [derivative](/wiki/Derivative_(finance) "Derivative (finance)") [financial products](/wiki/Financial_services "Financial services").[[17]](#cite_note-Lopatto-17)

The [real estate industry](/wiki/Real_estate_business "Real estate business"), including [asset managers](/wiki/Asset_management "Asset management"), [public companies](/wiki/Public_company "Public company") and [private investors](/wiki/Private_investors "Private investors"), has also invested in AI development.[[35]](#cite_note-35)

## Energy sourcing

This section is an excerpt from [Data center § Energy use and environmental impact](/wiki/Data_center#Energy_use_and_environmental_impact "Data center").[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Data_center&action=edit)]

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/2015-_Data_center_power_demand_-_US.svg/250px-2015-_Data_center_power_demand_-_US.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:2015-_Data_center_power_demand_-_US.svg)

Fueled by growth in artificial intelligence, data centers' demand for power increased in the 2020s.[[36]](#cite_note-Data_center_WashPost_20251225-36)

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Google_Data_Center%2C_The_Dalles.jpg/250px-Google_Data_Center%2C_The_Dalles.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Google_Data_Center,_The_Dalles.jpg)

[Google Data Center](/wiki/Google_Data_Centers "Google Data Centers"), [The Dalles, Oregon](/wiki/The_Dalles,_Oregon "The Dalles, Oregon")

Energy consumption and the environmental impacts it creates are a central issue for data centers.[[37]](#cite_note-37)[[38]](#cite_note-38) Power draw ranges from a few kilowatts (kW) for small server racks to several tens of megawatts (MW) for large facilities. Modern hyperscale data centers can exhibit power densities exceeding 100 times those of conventional office buildings, primarily due to the high concentration of servers and cooling systems required to manage continuous digital workloads.[[39]](#cite_note-39) For higher power density facilities, electricity costs are a dominant [operating expense](/wiki/Operating_expense "Operating expense") and account for over 10% of the [total cost of ownership](/wiki/Total_cost_of_ownership "Total cost of ownership") (TCO) of a data center.[[40]](#cite_note-40)

As of 2024, data centers in the U.S. are primarily powered by natural gas, which supplies 40% of their electricity (with [renewable energy](/wiki/Renewable_energy "Renewable energy") at 24%, nuclear at about 20%, and coal at about 15%).[[41]](#cite_note-Data_center_:03-41) The *[Associated Press](/wiki/Associated_Press "Associated Press")* reported that electricity for AI data centers in the U.S. would likely come from natural gas or oil, as companies prefer using currently available power plants, which primarily use fossil fuels. Fossil energy is also often cheaper in locations where data centers are developed, and experts believe that energy demands from [generative AI](/wiki/Generative_AI "Generative AI") and data centers would be difficult to fulfill with renewable energy alone. Some companies such as Google, Amazon, and Meta have expressed interest in [nuclear power](/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power") for their data centers.[[42]](#cite_note-Data_center_:7-42) As of 2020, according to the [IEA](/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency"), solar [photovoltaic](/wiki/Photovoltaics "Photovoltaics")-generated electricity is at its lowest cost in history.[[43]](#cite_note-43) Other data centers, including xAI's [Colossus](/wiki/Colossus_(supercomputer) "Colossus (supercomputer)"), OpenAI's [Stargate](/wiki/Stargate_LLC "Stargate LLC"), and Meta's Prometheus use their own off-grid [natural gas plants](/wiki/Gas-fired_power_plant "Gas-fired power plant").[[44]](#cite_note-Data_center_:32-44)[[45]](#cite_note-Data_center_:8-45) [Electric vehicle](/wiki/Electric_vehicle_battery "Electric vehicle battery") and [lithium-ion batteries](/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery "Lithium-ion battery") have also been used for powering data centers, including for Colossus.[[46]](#cite_note-46)[[47]](#cite_note-Data_center_:21-47)

[Power utility](/wiki/Electric_utility "Electric utility") companies make upgrades to their infrastructure to handle demands of new data centers, and the price for these changes typically falls on residential or smaller commercial consumers.[[41]](#cite_note-Data_center_:03-41)[[42]](#cite_note-Data_center_:7-42)[[48]](#cite_note-Data_center_:10-48) In 2025, the [Mountain Valley Pipeline](/wiki/Mountain_Valley_Pipeline "Mountain Valley Pipeline") announced plans to expand its capacity by 25% to meet energy needs for data centers.[[49]](#cite_note-Data_center_:6-49)

In December 2025, the [Federal Energy Regulatory Commission](/wiki/Federal_Energy_Regulatory_Commission "Federal Energy Regulatory Commission") (FERC) published a unanimous order allowing data centers in the U.S. to have a direct connection with [power plants](/wiki/Power_plants "Power plants").[[50]](#cite_note-50) United States Secretary of Energy [Chris Wright](/wiki/Chris_Wright "Chris Wright") expressed support for un-retiring coal plants to power AI data centers.[[51]](#cite_note-51) Electricity demands from AI data centers have slowed or reversed the retirement of [peaking power plants](/wiki/Peaking_power_plant "Peaking power plant") in the U.S.[[52]](#cite_note-Data_center_:22-52) For example, in 2025, [Southern Company](/wiki/Southern_Company "Southern Company") announced that energy use from data centers would prevent the company from retiring coal-fired power plants as it had earlier promised.[[49]](#cite_note-Data_center_:6-49) In [Nevada](/wiki/Nevada "Nevada"), the [Desert Research Institute](/wiki/Desert_Research_Institute "Desert Research Institute") (DRI) calculated that 35% of the state's energy production could go to data centers by the year 2030 if all projects planned in the state as of 2026 are completed.[[53]](#cite_note-Data_center_:15-53) DRI also stated, in areas where there are clusters of data centers, local consumers may end up paying the extra cost of expanding infrastructure.[[53]](#cite_note-Data_center_:15-53)

## Environmental footprint

See also: [Environmental impact of artificial intelligence](/wiki/Environmental_impact_of_artificial_intelligence "Environmental impact of artificial intelligence")

Average AI data centers have an electricity footprint equivalent to 100,000 households, and use billions of gallons of water for cooling their hardware.[[54]](#cite_note-54) In 2025, the [International Energy Agency](/wiki/International_Energy_Agency "International Energy Agency") estimated that the larger AI data centers currently under construction could consume as much electricity as 2 million households.[[9]](#cite_note-Leppert-9) A 2024 report from the [United States Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy") stated that data centers overall used 17 billion gallons of water per year in the United States, primarily due to "rapid proliferation of AI servers", and that this usage was forecasted to grow to nearly 80 billion gallons by 2028.[[33]](#cite_note-Kerr-33) Researchers estimated that AI data centers in the United States would emit 24–44 million metric tons of carbon dioxide and use 731–1,125 million cubic meters of water per year between 2024 and 2030.[[55]](#cite_note-55)

Peaking power plants, which have been proposed as a power source for AI data centers, emit [sulfur dioxide](/wiki/Sulfur_dioxide "Sulfur dioxide") and have historically been located disproportionately near communities of color in the United States.[[56]](#cite_note-56)

Reciprocating internal combustion engines, proposed as another power source for a data center, emit [PM 2.5](/wiki/Particulate_matter "Particulate matter"), [nitrogen oxides](/wiki/Nitrogen_oxides "Nitrogen oxides"), and [volatile organic compounds](/wiki/Volatile_organic_compound "Volatile organic compound").[[57]](#cite_note-57)

## Global memory supply shortage

Main article: [2024–2026 global memory supply shortage](/wiki/2024%E2%80%932026_global_memory_supply_shortage "2024–2026 global memory supply shortage")

The construction of AI data centers during the AI boom starting in 2024 has exhausted the global supply of the computer memory. To meet demand from AI servers, the three major DRAM manufacturers, [Samsung](/wiki/Samsung "Samsung"), [SK Hynix](/wiki/SK_Hynix "SK Hynix"), and [Micron](/wiki/Micron_Technology "Micron Technology"), prioritized production of [High Bandwidth Memory](/wiki/High_Bandwidth_Memory "High Bandwidth Memory") (HBM) and high-end server DRAM, contributing to a global memory supply shortage and intensifying competition for advanced memory chips.[[3]](#cite_note-reuters-ai-frenzy-3) Because HBM consumes far more fabrication capacity than conventional memory—producing a single bit of HBM requires roughly three times the wafer capacity of DDR5.[[58]](#cite_note-58)

The resulting price increases and shortages have affected computer memory broadly, including HBM, [DRAM](/wiki/Dynamic_random-access_memory "Dynamic random-access memory"), and [NAND flash memory](/wiki/NAND_flash_memory "NAND flash memory").[[3]](#cite_note-reuters-ai-frenzy-3)[[59]](#cite_note-59)[[60]](#cite_note-60)

Under an agreement with Samsung and SK Hynix, the [Stargate](/wiki/Stargate_LLC "Stargate LLC") project alone is set to receive 900,000 DRAM wafers per month, roughly 40% of total global DRAM production.[[61]](#cite_note-61)

## In the United States

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Data_center_infrastructure_in_the_United_States.jpg/250px-Data_center_infrastructure_in_the_United_States.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Data_center_infrastructure_in_the_United_States.jpg)

Data center infrastructure in the United States (2025)

In the United States, both the [Biden administration](/wiki/Presidency_of_Joe_Biden "Presidency of Joe Biden") and [second Trump administration](/wiki/Second_presidency_of_Donald_Trump "Second presidency of Donald Trump") supported the construction of AI data centers. In January 2025, then-president Joe Biden signed an executive order for federal government agencies to support AI data centers on federal sites built by private companies, study their effect on energy prices, and encourage their use of renewable energy.[[62]](#cite_note-62) In April 2025, the [United States Department of Energy](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy") suggested 16 possible sites, including [Los Alamos National Laboratory](/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory "Los Alamos National Laboratory"), [Sandia National Laboratories](/wiki/Sandia_National_Laboratories "Sandia National Laboratories") and [Oak Ridge National Laboratory](/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Laboratory "Oak Ridge National Laboratory").[[63]](#cite_note-63) In its July 2025 AI Action Plan, the second Trump administration supported increased production of AI data centers.[[64]](#cite_note-64)

Several US states have incentivized local data center construction.[[65]](#cite_note-65) For example, in 2024, lawmakers in Michigan approved tax breaks for data center equipment and construction material.[[66]](#cite_note-Larson-66) Some data center companies have also invested or promised to invest in the infrastructure of local communities.[[67]](#cite_note-Dellinger-67)[[68]](#cite_note-68)

In December 2025, [Democratic](/wiki/Democratic_Party_(United_States) "Democratic Party (United States)") senators [Elizabeth Warren](/wiki/Elizabeth_Warren "Elizabeth Warren"), [Chris Van Hollen](/wiki/Chris_Van_Hollen "Chris Van Hollen"), and [Richard Blumenthal](/wiki/Richard_Blumenthal "Richard Blumenthal") wrote to seven technology companies (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, CoreWeave, Digital Realty, and [Equinix](/wiki/Equinix "Equinix")) that they will investigate the effects of those companies' operations on consumer energy bills, highlighting AI data centers in particular.[[69]](#cite_note-69) That same month, 25 Democratic lawmakers wrote to the inspector general of the [United States Department of Commerce](/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Commerce "United States Department of Commerce") over possible [conflict of interest](/wiki/Conflict_of_interest "Conflict of interest") concerns involving secretary of commerce [Howard Lutnick](/wiki/Howard_Lutnick "Howard Lutnick") and AI data centers. Lutnick met with a co-founder of Fermi, which proposed an AI data center project financed by [Cantor Fitzgerald](/wiki/Cantor_Fitzgerald "Cantor Fitzgerald") and [Newmark Group](/wiki/Newmark_Group "Newmark Group").[[70]](#cite_note-70)

AI Infrastructure Coalition (AIIC), an organization led by Brian O. Walsh, [Kyrsten Sinema](/wiki/Kyrsten_Sinema "Kyrsten Sinema"), and [Garret Graves](/wiki/Garret_Graves "Garret Graves"), has supported Trump's AI Action Plan,[[71]](#cite_note-Allen-71) with Sinema becoming personally involved in a local meeting on an AI data center in Arizona.[[72]](#cite_note-Plautz-72) Venture capital firm [Andreessen Horowitz](/wiki/Andreessen_Horowitz "Andreessen Horowitz"), Big Tech companies (Google, Meta, Microsoft), telecommunications companies ([Cisco](/wiki/Cisco "Cisco"), [Corning Inc.](/wiki/Corning_Inc. "Corning Inc."), [Lumen Technologies](/wiki/Lumen_Technologies "Lumen Technologies")), data center providers (Digital Realty, QTS), energy companies ([Duke Energy](/wiki/Duke_Energy "Duke Energy"), [Entergy](/wiki/Entergy "Entergy"), ExxonMobil, NextEra Energy), and utility companies ([PG&E](/wiki/Pacific_Gas_and_Electric_Company "Pacific Gas and Electric Company"), [Pinnacle West Capital](/wiki/Pinnacle_West_Capital "Pinnacle West Capital")) are members of AIIC as of November 2025.[[71]](#cite_note-Allen-71) AIIC has been supported by [Hogan Lovells](/wiki/Hogan_Lovells "Hogan Lovells").[[72]](#cite_note-Plautz-72)

According to [Goldman Sachs](/wiki/Goldman_Sachs "Goldman Sachs") in 2025 an addition half million workers will be needed by 2030 to build the power infrastructure, which is acute because of AI data center build out.[[73]](#cite_note-73)[[74]](#cite_note-74)[[75]](#cite_note-75)

### Concerns and opposition

Main article: [Opposition to AI data centers](/wiki/Opposition_to_AI_data_centers "Opposition to AI data centers")

Some analysts have expressed concerns about overbuilding of AI data centers, warning that their infrastructure risks [obsolescence](/wiki/Technological_obsolescence "Technological obsolescence") due to changes in demand and technology.[[26]](#cite_note-Berg-26) Estimates for the lifespan of the GPUs used to power data centers range from one to eight years.[[76]](#cite_note-76)[[77]](#cite_note-77) By contrast, CPUs in more traditional data centers have a shelf life of about 5–7 years. Machine learning model training causes significant stress on computer chips.[[78]](#cite_note-78)

[![Image](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/Anti_ai_data_center_sign_2.jpg/250px-Anti_ai_data_center_sign_2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](/wiki/File:Anti_ai_data_center_sign_2.jpg)

Anti-data-center sentiment in rural [Kansas](/wiki/Kansas "Kansas") neighborhood

Advocates have also linked data center construction to the [AI bubble](/wiki/AI_bubble "AI bubble").[[79]](#cite_note-79)[[80]](#cite_note-80) [Wisconsin Watch](/wiki/Wisconsin_Center_for_Investigative_Journalism "Wisconsin Center for Investigative Journalism") expressed concern that data centers could become [stranded assets](/wiki/Stranded_assets "Stranded assets") in the context of an AI bubble, leaving energy customers to pay for the costs.[[81]](#cite_note-81) Journalists have warned that the large number of links between actors and organizations in the AI ecosystem[[17]](#cite_note-Lopatto-17) as well as the large number of companies involved[[29]](#cite_note-Nicoletti-29) each indicate strong risks for the [global economy](/wiki/World_economy "World economy") and [financial markets](/wiki/Financial_market "Financial market").

A hedge fund founder questioned the economics of AI data centers, concluding that they need about $1 trillion of revenue for profitability.[[82]](#cite_note-82)

In November 2025, the [North American Electric Reliability Corporation](/wiki/North_American_Electric_Reliability_Corporation "North American Electric Reliability Corporation") warned that building new data centers could negatively affect the [electrical grid](/wiki/Electrical_grid "Electrical grid") and cause power outages during [extreme weather](/wiki/Extreme_weather "Extreme weather").[[83]](#cite_note-FT-83) The independent monitor of [PJM Interconnection](/wiki/PJM_Interconnection "PJM Interconnection") warned that its [power grid](/wiki/Electrical_grid "Electrical grid") cannot support new data centers and supported a federal moratorium on data centers.[[24]](#cite_note-Halper-24)

Machine learning developers, when [training](/wiki/Model_training "Model training") [large language models](/wiki/Large_language_model "Large language model"), often use data centers at their full capacity, conflicting with other users (including households) during peak usage, which may lead to [blackouts](/wiki/Power_outage "Power outage").[[83]](#cite_note-FT-83)

Power disruptions in data centers can cause errors in machine learning model calculations ("silent data corruption").[[84]](#cite_note-84)

Large technology companies who are building data centers have asked public officials and land owners to sign [non-disclosure agreements](/wiki/Non-disclosure_agreement "Non-disclosure agreement") and have appeared to use [shell companies](/wiki/Shell_corporation "Shell corporation").[[85]](#cite_note-85) In one case, when a joint OpenAI-Oracle data center was rejected by [Saline Township](/wiki/Saline_Township,_Michigan "Saline Township, Michigan") in Michigan, landowners and developers responded by suing the town.[[66]](#cite_note-Larson-66)[[86]](#cite_note-86) The developers acquired the land from the resulting settlement. The [Michigan Public Service Commission](/wiki/Michigan_Public_Service_Commission "Michigan Public Service Commission") approved [DTE Energy](/wiki/DTE_Energy "DTE Energy") to provide power to the data center, part of Stargate. The approval was part of an [ex parte](/wiki/Ex_parte "Ex parte") motion, with no public input.[[67]](#cite_note-Dellinger-67)

In 2025, over 230 groups have signed a letter supporting a moratorium on constructing AI data centers in the United States, concerned by impact on the environment and energy bills. Signatories include [Food & Water Watch](/wiki/Food_%26_Water_Watch "Food & Water Watch"), [Greenpeace](/wiki/Greenpeace "Greenpeace"), [Friends of the Earth](/wiki/Friends_of_the_Earth "Friends of the Earth") and [Physicians for Social Responsibility](/wiki/Physicians_for_Social_Responsibility "Physicians for Social Responsibility").[[87]](#cite_note-87) Senator [Bernie Sanders](/wiki/Bernie_Sanders "Bernie Sanders") has also supported a moratorium on AI data centers.[[88]](#cite_note-88)

Ashley LaMont, of [Honor the Earth](/wiki/Honor_the_Earth "Honor the Earth"), argued that data centers on tribal lands would not help Native Americans obtain [data sovereignty](/wiki/Data_sovereignty "Data sovereignty").[[89]](#cite_note-89)

The [NAACP](/wiki/NAACP "NAACP") has also expressed opposition to AI data centers.[[90]](#cite_note-90)

## In space

See also: [Space-based data center](/wiki/Space-based_data_center "Space-based data center")

Several tech companies, including [Starcloud](/wiki/Starcloud "Starcloud"), Google, Nvidia, [Blue Origin](/wiki/Blue_Origin "Blue Origin") and [SpaceX](/wiki/SpaceX "SpaceX"), have announced projects for or otherwise expressed interest in building data centers in outer space.[[91]](#cite_note-91)[[92]](#cite_note-92)[[93]](#cite_note-93)[[94]](#cite_note-94) Google announced Project Suncatcher, which plans to test whether its [Tensor Processing Unit](/wiki/Tensor_Processing_Unit "Tensor Processing Unit") (TPU) chips (which are specifically designed for machine learning) can function in the environment of space. The company aims to deploy satellites with these chips in a data center by 2027.[[95]](#cite_note-95) In November 2025, Starcloud, a startup supported by Nvidia, launched a satellite with an [Nvidia H100 GPU](/wiki/Hopper_(microarchitecture) "Hopper (microarchitecture)") that the company used to deploy and develop a large language model. [CNBC](/wiki/CNBC "CNBC") reported that this model, NanoGPT, was the first model trained in space. Starcloud deployed a second model based on Google's [Gemma](/wiki/Gemma_(language_model) "Gemma (language model)").[[96]](#cite_note-96)
